Phishing

Not every trap looks like one

Phishing doesn't rely on breaking through firewalls or exploiting complex vulnerabilities — it relies on you. A convincing email, a familiar logo, a sense of urgency, and suddenly a fake login page looks exactly like the real thing.

It's one of the most common cyberattacks precisely because it's so simple. No advanced hacking skills required — just a well-crafted message and a moment of inattention. That's what makes it dangerous: it doesn't target your computer, it targets you.

Below, we've broken down what a real phishing attempt looks like, region by region, so you can start spotting the red flags before you click.

Types of phishing

Attackers pick their channel to fit the target.
Here are the ones you're most likely to run into.

Mass emails impersonating a bank, delivery service, or workplace tool,
pushing you to click a link or open an attachment before you stop to think.

A targeted message written for one specific person, using their name,
role, or a real coworker's identity to feel personal and trustworthy.

The same trick delivered by text message,
often a fake package delivery notice or bank alert with a shortened link.

A phone call from someone posing as tech support, a bank, or the IRS,
pressuring you to share account details or a one-time code out loud.

Spot the red flags

Most phishing attempts share a handful of tells once you know where to look.

Sender address

Looks close to real, but the domain is slightly off.

Urgency

"Act now or your account will be locked."

Generic greeting

"Dear customer" instead of your actual name.

Mismatched link

Hover text shows a domain that doesn't match.

Odd attachment

An invoice or document you never asked for.

Asking for info

Requests a password, PIN, or code over email.

Anatomy of a phishing email

Here's a mock example annotated with the tells from above.

From: security@paypa1-support.com sender
Subject: Your account will be suspended in 24 hours urgency
Dear valued customer, greeting
We noticed unusual activity on your account.
Verify your identity here: paypa1-secure-login.net link
Please confirm your password and card PIN below. info

If you clicked, here's what to do

Falling for one doesn't make you careless. Acting fast is what limits the damage.

1
Disconnect

Get off the wifi or unplug the cable if you downloaded anything.

2
Change your passwords

Start with the account that was impersonated, then any account reusing that password.

3
Turn on two-factor authentication

Adds a second lock the attacker doesn't have the key to.

4
Run a security scan

Check the device for malware, especially if you opened an attachment.

5
Report it

Flag it to your IT team, your bank, or reportphishing@apwg.org.

6
Watch your accounts

Keep an eye on statements and login activity for the next few weeks.
